    Discover what the instruments wrote

    Raw files are the source of truth. A scan of data/raw/ matches <sample>_ZEM.txt and <sample>_LFA.csv|xlsx and creates any missing batch or sample record.

  2. 02

    Merge without overwriting

    Rescans only update structural fields: file paths and whether a processed table exists. Chemistry I curated by hand is carried over untouched.

  3. 03

    Edit it like a lab notebook

    The database exports to one Markdown ledger, grouped by batch. Compositions, densities, heat capacity and annealing notes are edited in plain tables; file paths stay read-only.

    python main.py sync markdown export
  4. 04

    Round-trip back to typed JSON

    Importing parses each field by type: blank numbers become null, comma lists become arrays. Deleting a section never deletes a record unless I pass --prune.

    python main.py sync markdown
  5. 05

    Normalize literature tables

    Papers report mΩ·cm, S/cm, µV/K or µW cm−1 K−2. Headers are matched and scaled to SI, and anything not tabulated (σ, PF, zT, Lorenz, κL, weighted mobility) is derived.

    python main.py sync reference-xlsx
  6. 06

    One record shape, two sources

    A literature sample uses the same fields as a lab sample, plus where it came from. Compositions parsed from sample names are flagged for checking before they are used.

def discover_raw_samples(raw_dir):
    discovered = {}
    for batch_dir in sorted(p for p in raw_dir.iterdir() if p.is_dir()):
        for raw_file in sorted(batch_dir.iterdir()):
            match = RAW_FILE_PATTERN.match(raw_file.name)
            if not match:
                continue
            sample = discovered.setdefault(match["sample_id"], {
                "sample_id": match["sample_id"], "batch_id": batch_dir.name})
            sample[match["kind"].lower()] = relative_path(raw_file)
    return discovered

01 / Thermoelectric applications

Direct conversion between
heat and electricity

A Peltier cooler pumps heat with an electrical current and no moving parts, which makes it a candidate for cooling the hottest spots on a chip. The Seebeck effect runs the same physics in reverse to generate power.

01 Chip cooling 02 Effects & figure of merit 03 Cooling efficiency 04 Module cooling power 05 Power generation

01 / Why it matters now

Chips need cooling that is fast, stable and targeted

A modern system-on-chip packs CPU, GPU and AI cores into a few square centimetres, and its power is not spread evenly. A core at full load forms a hotspot whose local heat flux is far above the die average, and the whole chip throttles once that spot reaches its temperature limit. Fans, heat pipes and liquid loops cool the package as a whole; a thermoelectric cooler can pump heat away from one spot, on demand.[1][2]

Fast

Follows the load

Heat pumping tracks the drive current, so the cooler can react to a burst of computation as it happens. Thin films respond faster still, because heat crosses only micrometres of material.

Stable

Holds a set temperature

No moving parts, refrigerant or vibration. Reversing the current heats instead of cools, so closed-loop control can hold a temperature steady, as is standard for laser diodes and sensors.

Targeted

Cools one spot

A cooler the size of a core sits only where heat is generated, and each one can be driven separately, instead of over-cooling the whole package to protect its hottest point.

Bulk modules reach a maximum cooling flux of only about 10 W cm⁻²; thin-film superlattice modules have reached 258 W cm⁻², approaching what hotspots demand.[3] Whether active cooling pays off depends on COP and module design, explored below.

The plots use an ideal, single-stage, constant-property model. Device ZT̄ represents the operating temperature interval; it is not a material’s peak zT.[4][5][6]

From the model to an application

Solid-state cooling. Heat must be rejected at the hot side, including the electrical input: Qh = Qc + Pin. On a chip or on skin, the thermal interfaces and the heat sink matter as much as the cooler.[7]

Battery-free electronics. Body heat or another available heat source can supply low-power sensors when the harvested energy meets the load. The temperature difference across the device, power conversion and sensing duty cycle matter.[8]

Model assumptions and notation

Steady-state, one-dimensional, single-stage transport with constant α, R and K over each temperature interval. Ideal contacts; no parasitic heat leaks, Thomson heat, or heat-exchanger losses. Joule heat is split equally between the two ends. Temperatures are absolute (K), and ΔT = Th − Tc.

For one p–n couple, α = Sp − Sn, R = Rp + Rn, and K = Kp + Kn. A module uses effective totals. K (W K⁻¹) is thermal conductance; κ (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) is material thermal conductivity.

In the module plot, α, R and K are built from N couples of Bi₂Te₃-like legs (S = ±200 μV K⁻¹, ρ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ Ω m, κ = 1.5 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, so zT ≈ 0.8 at 300 K), with an electrical contact resistivity of 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ Ω m² at each leg end and legs covering 30% of the footprint. Thermal contact resistance and ceramic plates are neglected. The material properties are held at their room-temperature values across the 300–400 K hot-side range; real Bi₂Te₃ zT changes with temperature, so readings at high Th are indicative. The thin-film design keeps the same bulk properties to isolate the effect of geometry; real superlattice films differ. In the efficiency sweeps, ZT̄ is an independent effective parameter at each condition. The ΔT sweep at fixed ZT̄ compares idealized conditions, not the temperature dependence of one specific material. All curves are calculated examples, not measurements of the Ag₂(S,Se,Te) materials.

02 / Mechanics & flexibility

Mechanics shapes what
a semiconductor can do.

What determines whether a semiconductor device can be manufactured intact, operate reliably, and work in a new environment? Alongside its electronic and thermal properties, its mechanical response is a key part of the answer.

01 Properties & device outcomes 02 Flexibility in use 03 Ag–S–Se–Te timeline

Part 01 / Why mechanical properties matter

From material response
to device outcomes.

Strength, elastic limits, plastic deformation, and resistance to fracture matter at different stages of a device’s life. No single mechanical metric describes them all.[1]

Make it

Fabrication yield

Cutting, thinning, handling, and assembly impose local stresses. Strength and flaw tolerance help determine whether a component survives processing.

Strength · toughness · processability
Keep it working

Operational stability

Thermal expansion and repeated mechanical loads can damage the semiconductor or its contacts. Stable operation requires both structural and functional integrity.

Elastic limits · cracks · fatigue
Put it to use

Application space

A rigid package, a skin-mounted sensor, and a formed foil need different responses: dimensional stability, reversible bending, or permanent shape change.

Stiffness · flexibility · plasticity

A common language

Read the stress–strain curve.

The curve separates how easily a material deforms, how much stress it supports, and whether that deformation is recoverable.

E Stiffness / Young’s modulus
The initial linear slope, E = dσ/dε. A larger modulus means more stress is needed for the same small elastic strain.
σel Elastic limit
The greatest stress that leaves no permanent strain after unloading. The usable elastic strain also depends on the modulus.
σy · σUTS Strength
Yield strength marks a defined onset of plastic flow; ultimate tensile strength is the peak engineering tensile stress. A high strength does not by itself imply high ductility.
εp Plasticity & ductility
Plasticity leaves permanent deformation after unloading. Ductility describes tensile plastic deformation; large compression strain alone does not establish it.

Energy absorption

Toughness

The area under a uniaxial stress–strain curve up to fracture represents absorbed energy per unit volume. Both the sustained stress and the available strain matter.

Crack resistance

Fracture toughness

Resistance to the growth of an existing crack is a separate property. KIC (MPa m1/2) requires a suitable fracture test; it cannot be read directly from this curve.

Material properties set part of the operating window. Defects, specimen size, loading mode, interfaces, and temperature also affect fabrication yield and device reliability.

Part 02 / Flexibility in real applications

need to do?

Material × geometry

A brittle material can still bend.

For a thin, homogeneous sheet of thickness h bent to radius R, reducing thickness lowers both the surface strain at a given radius and the force needed to bend it. This gives an elastic route to flexibility.[4]

What changes?

Geometry changes the bending response. The material remains intrinsically brittle. Elastic bending recovers on unloading; plastic deformation leaves a permanent shape change.

Model & assumptions +

For a homogeneous, free-standing sheet in elastic bending, the neutral plane lies at mid-thickness and εmax = h/(2R). Here, R is measured at that plane. The illustrative limit εlimit = 0.50% is held constant as thickness changes; equality marks the failure boundary.

At a chosen R, hc = 2Rεlimit. This is a geometry-dependent boundary, not a universal brittle-to-flexible transition. Above it, the schematic shows fracture before the target curvature is reached; the strain readout is the required target strain, not a post-fracture measurement. Changing thickness or radius starts a new specimen.

For fixed width, elastic modulus, and Poisson ratio, bending rigidity scales as h³. The visualization prescribes curvature rather than force. Real strength may also depend on thickness, surface damage, edge quality, and flaw statistics. Substrates and multilayers shift the neutral plane.

Peng and Snyder express flexibility as 1/Rmin = (2/h)(σy/E), with σy/E as the material figure of merit. For this brittle-Si illustration, εlimit denotes a fracture limit rather than plastic yielding.

From geometry to devices

Flexible silicon solar cells

Liu and colleagues demonstrated flexible solar cells using approximately 60 μm silicon wafers with blunted edges. Thinning and edge treatment work together: the latter reduces fracture initiation at sharp surface features near the wafer edge.

Material response / Intrinsic plasticity

The Ag2S family opens another route.

Room-temperature ductility in α-Ag2S showed that an inorganic semiconductor can undergo substantial plastic deformation. The reported bonding picture combines relatively weak interactions between planes with bonding rearrangements that suppress cleavage during slip.[6]

Alloying within silver chalcogenides then provides a way to connect deformability with thermoelectric transport. Ag2S-based materials have been used in flexible inorganic thermoelectric devices; my cubic Ag2(S,Se,Te) study extends the composition–structure–property design space.[7][3]

Plasticity depends on composition, phase, temperature, and loading. The ductility of one Ag2S-based material should not be assigned to every silver chalcogenide.

The mechanical evidence

More than a compression curve.

We measured compression and bending response, fracture toughness, and energy absorption across a series of Ag2Se1−xTex compositions. Ag2Se0.9Te0.1 reaches a compression strength of 279 ± 49 MPa alongside its large compressive strain.

Fracture toughness and energy absorption add complementary evidence of robustness; in bending, the alloys remain brittle.[1]

Microstructural insight

How is deformation accommodated?

TEM after compression reveals dense dislocations and dislocation arrays, and sub-grain boundary structures point to a possible contribution from sub-grain rotation.

Together they link the macroscopic response to deformation inside the grains.[1]

Thermoelectric transport

Useful performance near room temperature.

The nominal Ag2Se0.9Te0.1 composition reaches zT = 0.78 at 300 K and 1.1 at 380 K. Small Ag deficiencies, kept out of the nominal label, optimize the carrier concentration.

The result brings compressive plasticity and high fracture toughness into the same material design discussion as carrier transport and thermal conductivity.[1]

Composition & structure

Map the design space.

We compiled composition–structure–plasticity–zT maps across the pseudo-ternary alloy system and outlined the cubic region. The equimolar S/Se/Te anion composition provides a high-entropy starting point, while small Ag deficiencies tune carrier concentration.[1]

Intrinsic ductility

Large deformation in tension.

Ag2S1/3Se1/3Te1/3 reaches approximately 97% tensile elongation; Ag1.98S1/3Se1/3Te1/3 reaches approximately 60%. Necking, slip bands, and local strain evolution provide evidence of large plastic deformation.

This tensile response supports a different processing opportunity from the compressive plasticity of the Ag2Se-based alloys.[1]

Transport optimization

Tune carriers, retain ductility.

Ag deficiency lowers the carrier concentration and adjusts electrical and thermal transport. Ag1.98S1/3Se1/3Te1/3 reaches zT = 0.45 at 300 K and 0.8 at 460 K.

The room-temperature κ–σ analysis suggests an average lattice thermal conductivity of approximately 0.2 W m−1 K−1 in the studied cubic materials.[1]

From bulk material to a device

Roll, shape, and generate power.

Rolling produces foils down to 30 μm. A separate bending test on a 30 μm foil maintains R/R0 within 99.2–101.3% over 150 cycles, with a minimum bending radius of approximately 3 mm.

A six-leg in-plane generator uses 50 μm Ag1.98S1/3Se1/3Te1/3 foils, Pt wires, and Cu electrodes. In the laboratory at ΔT = 30 K, it delivers a maximum output power of 12.8 μW and a reported maximum power density of 14.3 W m−2.[1]
