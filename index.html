01 / Thermoelectric applications

Direct conversion between
heat and electricity

The Peltier effect enables solid-state cooling. The Seebeck effect converts a temperature difference into electrical power.

01 Effects & figure of merit 02 Cooling efficiency 03 Cooling power 04 Power generation

Thermoelectric effects and figure of merit

Peltier effect

Solid-state cooling

An electrical current pumps heat from the cold side to the hot side.

QPeltier=αTcI

Peltier heat is only one contribution to the net cooling power.

Seebeck effect

Power generation

A temperature difference generates a voltage; a connected load receives electrical power.

Voc=αΔT

Open-circuit voltage for a constant effective Seebeck coefficient α.

Material figure of merit

zT=S2σTκ=S2Tρκ

S: Seebeck coefficient · σ: electrical conductivity · ρ = 1/σ · κ: total thermal conductivity.

Device parameter used in the plots

ZT¯=α2T¯RK

T̄ = (Th + Tc)/2. α: effective Seebeck coefficient · R: electrical resistance · K: thermal conductance.

The plots use an ideal, single-stage, constant-property model. Device ZT̄ represents the operating temperature interval; it is not a material’s peak zT.[3][4][5]

From the model to an application

Solid-state cooling. Heat must be rejected at the hot side, including the electrical input: Qh = Qc + Pin. Wearable cooling also depends on thermal interfaces and the heat sink.[1]

Battery-free electronics. Body heat or another available heat source can supply low-power sensors when the harvested energy meets the load. The temperature difference across the device, power conversion and sensing duty cycle matter.[2]

Model assumptions and notation

Steady-state, one-dimensional, single-stage transport with constant α, R and K over each temperature interval. Ideal contacts; no parasitic heat leaks, Thomson heat, or heat-exchanger losses. Joule heat is split equally between the two ends. Temperatures are absolute (K), and ΔT = Th − Tc.

For one p–n couple, α = Sp − Sn, R = Rp + Rn, and K = Kp + Kn. A module uses effective totals. K (W K⁻¹) is thermal conductance; κ (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) is material thermal conductivity.

In the cooling-power plot, α, R and Th stay fixed; K and ΔT are adjustable, so ZT̄ is calculated. In the efficiency sweeps, ZT̄ is an independent effective parameter at each condition. The ΔT sweep at fixed ZT̄ compares idealized conditions, not the temperature dependence of one specific material. All curves are calculated examples, not measurements of the Ag₂(S,Se,Te) materials.

02 / Mechanics & flexibility

Mechanics shapes what
a semiconductor can do.

What determines whether a semiconductor device can be manufactured intact, operate reliably, and work in a new environment? Alongside its electronic and thermal properties, its mechanical response is a key part of the answer.

01 Properties & device outcomes 02 Flexibility in use 03 Ag–S–Se–Te timeline

Part 01 / Why mechanical properties matter

From material response
to device outcomes.

Strength, elastic limits, plastic deformation, and resistance to fracture matter at different stages of a device’s life. No single mechanical metric describes them all.[9]

Make it

Fabrication yield

Cutting, thinning, handling, and assembly impose local stresses. Strength and flaw tolerance help determine whether a component survives processing.

Strength · toughness · processability
Keep it working

Operational stability

Thermal expansion and repeated mechanical loads can damage the semiconductor or its contacts. Stable operation requires both structural and functional integrity.

Elastic limits · cracks · fatigue
Put it to use

Application space

A rigid package, a skin-mounted sensor, and a formed foil need different responses: dimensional stability, reversible bending, or permanent shape change.

Stiffness · flexibility · plasticity

A common language

Read the stress–strain curve.

The curve separates how easily a material deforms, how much stress it supports, and whether that deformation is recoverable.

E Stiffness / Young’s modulus
The initial linear slope, E = dσ/dε. A larger modulus means more stress is needed for the same small elastic strain.
σel Elastic limit
The greatest stress that leaves no permanent strain after unloading. The usable elastic strain also depends on the modulus.
σy · σUTS Strength
Yield strength marks a defined onset of plastic flow; ultimate tensile strength is the peak engineering tensile stress. A high strength does not by itself imply high ductility.
εp Plasticity & ductility
Plasticity leaves permanent deformation after unloading. Ductility describes tensile plastic deformation; large compression strain alone does not establish it.

Energy absorption

Toughness

The area under a uniaxial stress–strain curve up to fracture represents absorbed energy per unit volume. Both the sustained stress and the available strain matter.

Crack resistance

Fracture toughness

Resistance to the growth of an existing crack is a separate property. KIC (MPa m1/2) requires a suitable fracture test; it cannot be read directly from this curve.

Material properties set part of the operating window. Defects, specimen size, loading mode, interfaces, and temperature also affect fabrication yield and device reliability.

Part 02 / Flexibility in real applications

What does the device
need to do?

Flexibility connects mechanical properties to the shape and motion of an application. Start with the required deformation, then choose the material and architecture.

Conform & follow motion

Wearables & skin sensors

Fit a curved surface and move with the body while maintaining contact and electrical function.

Evaluate Bending radius, repeated cycles, contact stability, and output under motion.

Wrap a curved surface

Conformal solar cells

Use thin semiconductor sheets on curved supports. Edge and surface quality become part of the mechanical design.[4]

Evaluate Surface strain, edge defects, thermal cycling, and retained efficiency.

Form & retain a shape

Deformable TE foils

Roll or form a plastic semiconductor into a useful geometry, then operate it within an appropriate service window.[8]

Evaluate Forming strain, cracking, and transport properties after deformation.

Material × geometry

A brittle material can still bend.

For a thin, homogeneous sheet of thickness h bent to radius R, reducing thickness lowers both the surface strain at a given radius and the force needed to bend it. This gives an elastic route to flexibility.[3]

Surface strainεmax ≈ h / (2R)

Thinner sheet → less strain at the same radius.

Use the recoverable strain limit; fracture can set the limit in brittle materials.

Simple bending of a free-standing sheet; R is measured at the neutral plane. At fixed modulus and width, bending rigidity scales with h3.

What changes?

Geometry changes the bending response. The material remains intrinsically brittle. Elastic bending recovers on unloading; plastic deformation leaves a permanent shape change.

Model & assumptions +

For a homogeneous, free-standing sheet in elastic bending, the neutral plane lies at mid-thickness and εmax = h/(2R). Here, R is measured at that plane. The illustrative limit εlimit = 0.50% is held constant as thickness changes; equality marks the failure boundary.

At a chosen R, hc = 2Rεlimit. This is a geometry-dependent boundary, not a universal brittle-to-flexible transition. Above it, the schematic shows fracture before the target curvature is reached; the strain readout is the required target strain, not a post-fracture measurement. Changing thickness or radius starts a new specimen.

For fixed width, elastic modulus, and Poisson ratio, bending rigidity scales as h³. The visualization prescribes curvature rather than force. Real strength may also depend on thickness, surface damage, edge quality, and flaw statistics. Substrates and multilayers shift the neutral plane.

Peng and Snyder express flexibility as 1/Rmin = (2/h)(σy/E), with σy/E as the material figure of merit. For this brittle-Si illustration, εlimit denotes a fracture limit rather than plastic yielding.

From geometry to devices

Flexible silicon solar cells

Liu and colleagues demonstrated flexible solar cells using approximately 60 μm silicon wafers with blunted edges. Thinning and edge treatment work together: the latter reduces fracture initiation at sharp surface features near the wafer edge.

Liu et al. · Nature 617, 717–723 (2023) ↗

Material response / Intrinsic plasticity

The Ag2S family opens another route.

Room-temperature ductility in α-Ag2S showed that an inorganic semiconductor can undergo substantial plastic deformation. The reported bonding picture combines relatively weak interactions between planes with bonding rearrangements that suppress cleavage during slip.[5]

Alloying within silver chalcogenides then provides a way to connect deformability with thermoelectric transport. Ag2S-based materials have been used in flexible inorganic thermoelectric devices; my cubic Ag2(S,Se,Te) study extends the composition–structure–property design space.[6][8]

Plasticity depends on composition, phase, temperature, and loading. The ductility of one Ag2S-based material should not be assigned to every silver chalcogenide.

The mechanical evidence

More than a compression curve.

We measured compression and bending response, fracture toughness, and energy absorption across a series of Ag2Se1−xTex compositions. Ag2Se0.9Te0.1 reaches a compression strength of 279.2 ± 49 MPa alongside its large compressive strain.

Its fracture toughness and energy absorption provide complementary evidence of mechanical robustness.[7]

The alloys exhibit brittle behavior in bending. Compression plasticity is not evidence of equivalent tensile ductility.

Microstructural insight

How is deformation accommodated?

TEM after compression reveals dense dislocations and dislocation arrays. The observed sub-grain boundary structures also suggest a possible contribution from sub-grain rotation.

These observations connect the macroscopic response to deformation within the microstructure; sub-grain rotation remains a proposed mechanism.[7]

Thermoelectric transport

Useful performance near room temperature.

The nominal Ag2Se0.9Te0.1 composition reaches zT = 0.78 at 300 K and 1.1 at 380 K. Small Ag deficiencies are used to optimize transport; the paper retains nominal stoichiometric labels for readability.

The result brings compressive plasticity and high fracture toughness into the same material design discussion as carrier transport and thermal conductivity.[7]

Ductile cubic alloys.
Shapeable thermoelectrics.

High-entropy design in the pseudo-ternary Ag₂S–Ag₂Se–Ag₂Te system connects cubic crystal structure, tensile ductility, and thermoelectric transport. The materials can be shaped and roller-pressed into flexible foils.

Composition & structure

Map the design space.

We compiled composition–structure–plasticity–zT maps across the pseudo-ternary alloy system and outlined the cubic region. The equimolar S/Se/Te anion composition provides a high-entropy starting point, while small Ag deficiencies tune carrier concentration.[8]

The 15% bending strain in the phase map is restricted by the test geometry for ductile compositions; it is not an ultimate fracture strain.

Intrinsic ductility

Large deformation in tension.

Ag2S1/3Se1/3Te1/3 reaches approximately 97% tensile elongation; Ag1.98S1/3Se1/3Te1/3 reaches approximately 60%. Necking, slip bands, and local strain evolution provide evidence of large plastic deformation.

This tensile response supports a different processing opportunity from the compressive plasticity of the Ag2Se-based alloys.[8]

Transport optimization

Tune carriers, retain ductility.

Ag deficiency lowers the carrier concentration and adjusts electrical and thermal transport. Ag1.98S1/3Se1/3Te1/3 reaches zT = 0.45 at 300 K and 0.8 at 460 K.

The room-temperature κ–σ analysis suggests an average lattice thermal conductivity of approximately 0.2 W m−1 K−1 in the studied cubic materials.[8]

From bulk material to a device

Roll, shape, and generate power.

Rolling produces foils down to 30 μm. A separate bending test on a 30 μm foil maintains R/R0 within 99.2–101.3% over 150 cycles, with a minimum bending radius of approximately 3 mm.

A six-leg in-plane generator uses 50 μm Ag1.98S1/3Se1/3Te1/3 foils, Pt wires, and Cu electrodes. At ΔT = 30 K, it delivers a maximum output power of 12.8 μW and a reported maximum power density of 14.3 W m−2.[8]

This is a laboratory device demonstration at the reported temperature difference. Its output is not an on-body power measurement or a demonstrated battery-free sensor system.

About me

I am a Research Fellow at Nanyang Technological University and a Visiting Postdoctoral Fellow at Northwestern University. My research connects materials synthesis, transport physics, and mechanical properties.

My work spans high-entropy thermoelectric materials, ductile inorganic semiconductors, and flexible devices. I also develop TEDEX, a workspace for experimental data management, transport modeling, scientific plotting, and AI-assisted materials screening.
